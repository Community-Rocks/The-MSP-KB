# Choosing the right tools

MSPs commonly use an RMM and a PSA alongside documentation, security, backup, networking, and reporting tools. Selecting one product is therefore usually a stack-design decision rather than a feature-counting exercise. A capability that looks complete in isolation may still create duplicate work, weak handoffs, or gaps when it is placed into the MSP's day-to-day service workflow.

The factors MSPs commonly compare include:

* **Workflow and integration fit:** An MSP may prefer an RMM that creates useful PSA tickets, carries customer and device context into the ticket, and returns the resolution to the original alert. Documentation, identity, security, and billing integrations also affect how much manual re-entry remains.
* **Required service coverage:** The useful feature set depends on the services the MSP actually delivers. Endpoint monitoring, patching, scripting, remote support, network visibility, reporting, and backup may be combined in one platform or intentionally split across specialist tools.
* **Customer and permission boundaries:** Multi-tenant separation, role design, approval controls, multifactor authentication, and technician access reviews influence whether a tool fits the MSP's customer and security model.
* **Operational evidence:** MSPs often need more than a successful button click. Job results, patch outcomes, alert history, technician activity, exports, and audit records help the team explain what happened and verify that a workflow completed.
* **Scalability and performance:** The platform must remain usable as customer, site, endpoint, technician, ticket, and automation counts grow. Pricing may scale along a different dimension from the technical workload.
* **Usability and adoption:** A capable tool still creates risk if technicians avoid it, cannot understand its state, or need undocumented workarounds. Training effort, interface consistency, and the clarity of failure messages affect adoption.
* **Support and vendor operations:** Support hours, escalation paths, maintenance communication, product-change practices, and the availability of status and security information all shape the operating relationship.
* **Total cost and contract shape:** MSPs compare the base subscription with technician, endpoint, storage, integration, implementation, support, and retained-tool costs. Minimum commitments, renewal terms, and price-adjustment rules can matter as much as the headline rate.
* **Implementation and exit requirements:** A representative pilot can expose deployment, policy, performance, and integration problems before a fleet-wide rollout. Data export, agent removal, credential rotation, rollback, and customer-offboarding paths determine how safely the MSP can later change course.

## How evaluations are commonly recorded

Many MSPs begin with a small set of representative customers or noncritical endpoints. The team records the required outcome, the evidence produced, any boundary or exception, the person responsible for remediation, and the retest result. This makes different products easier to compare and prevents an attractive total score from hiding a failed security or removal control.

One example is the [RMM Evaluation Scorecard Template](https://izikipizi.github.io/nizlo-msp-operations-resources/rmm-evaluation-scorecard-template.html), a blank, vendor-neutral CSV and evidence workflow maintained by Nizlo. It covers inventory, monitoring, patching, automation, remote support, permissions, audit evidence, recovery, removal, and technician usability. The template is a starting structure rather than an endorsement of any product or a substitute for the MSP's own requirements.
